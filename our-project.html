To study how sanctions are evaded, it helps to know how they are created and checked. This page explains the UN sanctions system and the Panels of Experts whose reports are the evidence base of EXPOSE.

In short

From a Security Council decision to a Panel report

Sanctions

The Security Council decides on measures to pressure a state, group or individual.

Committee

A sanctions committee of Council members oversees how the measures are applied.

Panel

Independent experts investigate violations of these sanctions on the ground.

Reports

The Panel reports its findings. EXPOSE reads and codes them.

1 · Background

What are UN sanctions?

UN sanctions are coercive measures decided by the UN Security Council to respond to threats to international peace and security, such as armed conflict, nuclear proliferation or terrorism. They aim to change the behaviour of a government, group or individual without the use of armed force.

Sanctions are targeted. Instead of cutting a whole country off, most regimes today focus on specific goods, sectors, entities and people.

Arms embargoesBans on selling or transferring weapons and related material.
Asset freezesFunds and property of listed persons or entities are frozen.
Travel bansListed individuals cannot enter or transit through states.
Commodity and sector bansRestrictions on goods such as coal, oil products, charcoal or luxury goods.
Financial restrictionsLimits on banking, financial services and transfers.
Other measuresFor example diplomatic or technical-cooperation limits.
2 · Process

How are sanctions imposed and monitored?

Imposing. Sanctions are adopted by a Security Council resolution, normally under Chapter VII of the UN Charter. A resolution needs nine votes out of fifteen and no veto from one of the five permanent members. The resolution sets the measures, who they apply to, how long they last and who will oversee them.

Binding force. Decisions of the Council are binding on all UN Member States. The UN has no police or customs of its own, so states must put the measures into national law and enforce them.

Monitoring. Because enforcement depends on states, the Council sets up bodies to follow implementation: a sanctions committee and, in many regimes, a Panel of Experts. Sanctions are reviewed regularly and can be renewed, changed or lifted.

Resolution adopted

The Council defines the measures, scope and duration.

States implement

Member States apply the measures through national laws and controls.

Committee and Panel follow

They collect information on implementation and on violations.

Council reviews

Findings inform renewals, new listings and adjustments.

3 · Institutions

What is a sanctions committee?

A sanctions committee is a subsidiary body of the Security Council created by a sanctions resolution. All fifteen Council members take part, and a chair and vice-chairs are drawn from among the elected members. Committees usually take decisions by consensus.

Its main tasks are to:

  • oversee how the sanctions measures are implemented;
  • keep and update the list of individuals and entities subject to sanctions;
  • consider requests for exemptions and delistings;
  • receive information from states and from the Panel of Experts;
  • report regularly to the Security Council.

Why it matters for EXPOSE. The committee is the political body that receives the Panel's reports. It is made up of states, while the Panel's experts are independent. That distinction explains why Panel reports can be more detailed and blunt than Council decisions.

4 · Institutions

What is a Panel of Experts?

A Panel of Experts (in some regimes called a Group of Experts or Monitoring Group) is a small team of independent specialists appointed to assist a sanctions committee. The Secretary-General appoints the experts at the request of the Council, and they usually serve for a mandate of about a year that the Council can renew.

Panels typically include experts in areas such as arms, finance, customs, maritime and aviation, regional affairs, and, more recently, cyber and virtual assets.

Their job is to:

  • gather, examine and analyse information on implementation of the sanctions;
  • investigate reported violations and evasion on the ground;
  • identify individuals and entities that may be violating the measures;
  • recommend how to improve implementation.

Not every sanctions regime has a Panel, and Panels can end. The DPRK Panel of Experts is no longer active: its mandate was not renewed in 2024, so its earlier reports remain the main public record for that regime.

5 · Evidence

What are the reports of the Panels of Experts?

Panels report to the sanctions committee and, through it, to the Security Council. The reports are public UN documents, identified by a symbol such as S/2019/691.

What they contain

Findings on violations and evasion, case studies, names of vessels, companies and networks involved, and recommendations for the Council, the committee and states.

When they are published

Usually a mid-term update and a final report during each mandate, plus occasional special reports. The committee and Council discuss them.

How they are built

Experts use field visits, state replies, open sources, commercial data and satellite or ship-tracking information, and they require evidence before reporting a finding.

Why they are valuable, and their limits. The reports are among the most detailed public records of how sanctions are evaded. They are also long, differ in structure from panel to panel, and cover only what experts could investigate and verify. Absence of a finding is not proof that no evasion took place.

What EXPOSE does with them. We read the reports, identify each documented evasion and code it, so patterns can be compared across regimes and over time. Explore the database →
